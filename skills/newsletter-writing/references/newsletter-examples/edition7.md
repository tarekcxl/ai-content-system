Title: Reverse-Engineering YouTube Growth With AI

If there’s one thing recent SEO and GEO studies have confirmed, it’s that YouTube is only getting more important.

It’s already one of the top domains referenced by LLMs. And according to a recent study from Datos, YouTube searches are still growing. Not slowing down.
In the US, it officially became the #1 external destination from Google Search. More users prefer watching the answer instead of reading it.




In other words: you can’t afford to ignore YouTube anymore.

That’s where AI comes in.
The opportunity isn’t just in creating more videos. It’s in doing better research. Understanding what works. Why does it work. And how to apply those insights to your own channel.

Let’s start with the first step.




Identifying what works for other channels

You can identify channels in your niche and analyze their recent videos to spot what’s performing well. The goal is simple: uncover which content pillars and topics are gaining traction, because those are exactly the themes you should consider creating videos about.

Our first approach was using ChatGPT Agent to visit a YouTube channel, extract recent videos, and generate insights. 


In theory, perfect. In reality? Not so much. The agent had difficulty extracting video data reliably and took an unusually long time to complete the task.

So we moved to Plan B.

We installed ChatGPT Atlas, OpenAI’s web browser, and manually navigated to a YouTube channel. For this example, we used the HubSpot Marketing Channel. 
This time, we were able to accurately extract recent video data and analyze performance patterns.

Here’s exactly how we did it.

We specifically asked it to analyze the most recent videos. Older videos that performed well in the past don’t always reflect what’s working right now. Reviewing recent uploads from competitors gives a much clearer signal of which topics and formats are currently resonating.

Next came the most important part: how we measured performance.

We didn’t look at average views. Averages are misleading, one viral video can inflate the number and give a false impression of a channel’s real baseline performance. Instead, we focused on median views, which gives a far more realistic picture of typical video performance.



Once we had the median, we classified recent videos into:

Breakout videos: Significantly above the median.
Solid performers: Moderately above the median.
Average performers: Around the median.
Underperformers: Below the median.

This classification lets us zoom in on breakout and solid performers, identify their content topics and pillars, and replicate what’s clearly working.





You also can spot recurring patterns in top videos’ thumbnails, one of the most important elements on YouTube. If your thumbnail doesn’t invite people to click, no one will watch your video.

Reverse-engineering successful videos 

Now that we know which content pillars and topics are performing well, the next step is understanding why certain videos outperform others.

Because it’s never just the topic.

Performance is also influenced by the video’s structure, script, visual storytelling, pacing, the presenter’s energy, presentation skills, animation style, and music. 
All the elements that make a video engaging to watch.

The challenge?
Most AI tools only summarize transcripts. They don’t actually watch the video.

But that’s changing.

Gemini 3 can now analyze videos directly. It doesn’t just read what’s said, it also interprets how it’s presented. And that unlocks an entirely new level of content analysis.

We first attempted to access it via the Gemini web app but the output lacked accuracy. It appeared the model wasn't fully processing the video content, leading to several hallucinations.

Next, we headed to Google’s AI Studio and opened a new prompt. This time, the output was accurate, consistent, and reliable. 

Please note: you’ll need to set up an API key and enable billing before getting started. Analyzing a short YouTube video typically costs only a few cents.



For this example, I tested this on one of my own videos, a video that outperformed my channel’s median views. It was about the Duolingo streak, packed with visual elements and audio cues. The goal was simple: see whether the tool could actually detect what made it work and explain why.



At the start of the video, I show a series of Reddit threads where people talk about struggling to maintain their streaks, paired with Queen’s Under Pressure as background music. And interestingly, Gemini flagged this exact sequence as a strong opening hook.


Another moment in the video compares retention to a leaking bucket of water and I visually demonstrate how that looks.


Gemini picked up on this as well, highlighting it as one of the video’s engaging visual elements.



One of the coolest parts of this analysis was seeing that Gemini didn’t just evaluate the video itself, it also analyzed how I show up on camera. 



It also pointed out what I did well, what could be replicated, and even highlighted missed opportunities.


That means you can use these insights to recreate successful formats, refine future videos, or improve your on-camera presence over time.

Next, I tested it on a demo-focused video to see if it could pick up instructional formats and offer useful advice. I used one of CXL’s YouTube videos that walks through creating AI powered B2B personas for LinkedIn.

It pinpointed what made the demo effective.





And where it could improve.

Tools are helpful but research is essential

When it comes to YouTube, it’s incredibly easy to get distracted by the endless wave of AI tools promising to help you create content. That’s a whole discussion on its own. With so many tools available, the risk is ending up with content that feels generic.

But in the process, we often overlook what’s actually most effective: solid research.

As this newsletter shows, taking the time to study what works, understand why it works, and then build your own version on top of those insights is still one of the strongest foundations you can have.
